# v15.1 Critique — Unified Design Philosophy

## Verdict

v15 is stronger than v14 overall and should remain the base document. It preserves the four-principle framework while adding useful empirical support, sharper limits, and better distinctions between enforcement, validation, control, and information. The five findings below do not reverse that verdict, but they should be corrected before the document is described as fully “evidence-hardened.”

This critique refers to the current line numbers in [v15](<15 - Unified Design Philosophy (6 - Evidence-Hardened by Opus 5).md>).

## 1. The trusted-refusal limit is contradicted by both the document and the implementation

**Location:** v15 lines 336–338 and 457.

v15 correctly observes that a Figma artifact can contain “text other people wrote,” including layer names, text, component descriptions, and collaborator notes. It then says the project does not face the trusted-refusal tension because refusals describe facts about “the user's own file.” Ownership is not a trust boundary: a shared or imported file can contain collaborator-authored or adversarial text.

The implementation makes the tension concrete. [`nodeReaders.ts`](../../figma_plugin/handlers/nodeReaders.ts) returns stored node names and other safelisted values; [`errors.ts`](../../figma_plugin/utils/errors.ts) and [`componentHandlers.ts`](../../figma_plugin/handlers/componentHandlers.ts) can place stored names into refusal text; and [`_result.ts`](../../src/mcp_server/tools/_result.ts) serializes plugin results into model-visible text. The project does not currently provide CaMeL-style separation between trusted instructions and untrusted artifact content.

This does not mean that such a defence must be added here. It means the limitation is outside the current threat model and unmitigated, not absent. The existing sentence also conflicts with Principle 4's own warning that artifact content can look like an instruction. See the [CaMeL evidence](../../EVIDENCE.md#counterevidence-enforcement-can-force-a-less-informative-refusal-camel) and the [primary paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.18813).

**Recommended replacement:**

> This project has not implemented CaMeL-style trust separation. The tension therefore remains outside the current threat model, not absent: artifact content returned in results or refusals may have been written by collaborators or imported from elsewhere.

## 2. Principle 3 conflates task time, successful-run time, and model-inference latency

**Location:** v15 lines 295–305.

The opening claim that three neighboring systems report “success and elapsed time together” is too broad:

- DMI reports wall-clock time only for successful cases: 392 seconds for GUI-only and 239 seconds for GUI+DMI. The successful-case filter matters because a system that succeeds on more difficult cases can change the population being timed.
- AutoDroid-V2 reports **LLM inference latency**, measured from receipt of the prompt to the final generated token. It does not report end-to-end task elapsed time for the quoted 5.7×–13.4× comparison.
- AXIS does report task-completion time for the quoted Word experiment: 59.5 seconds versus 29.9 seconds.

The section also calls AutoDroid-V2 and AXIS “independent replications,” although they use different systems, interventions, tasks, models, and timing measures. They are independent corroborations of a direction, not replications of one experiment. “With the confound removed” likewise overstates DMI's ablation: it tests the added-navigation-context explanation, but does not remove every alternative explanation. DMI's remaining failures also include ambiguous descriptions, control-semantics errors, and perception problems, so they should not all be classified as task-meaning ambiguity on the judgment side. Finally, the statement that “every system above” paid a substantial one-time modelling cost should be restricted to the three GUI systems; it is not established for Anthropic's programmatic-tool-calling mechanism.

The efficiency thesis remains well supported after these qualifications. The sources converge on higher success and lower cost or latency when already-determined work moves behind a higher-level interface; they simply do not measure one interchangeable form of elapsed time. See the [DMI, AutoDroid-V2, and AXIS evidence](../../EVIDENCE.md#faster-designing-tools-around-decisions) and the primary reports for [DMI](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.04607), [AutoDroid-V2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.18116), and [AXIS](https://aclanthology.org/2025.acl-long.381/).

**Recommended framing:**

> Three neighboring systems report convergent gains in success and some latency measures. DMI and AXIS report task time, although DMI calculates its timing metrics on successful cases only; AutoDroid-V2 reports model-inference latency. These are independent corroborations of the mechanism, not direct replications.

## 3. AbsenceBench supports marking gaps only by analogy

**Location:** v15 lines 332 and 364.

AbsenceBench gives the model an original document and an edited document side by side, then asks it to identify surface-form deletions. It shows that deleted content is harder to identify than inserted content and that placing a marker at the deletion site improves performance.

It does **not** test a silently truncated or paginated tool result, a model treating an incomplete list as exhaustive, or the downstream claim that the model will act on a confident false conclusion. Those are plausible interface-design inferences, but they are not directly measured by this benchmark. The current prose turns a useful analogy into evidence for a stronger causal chain than the experiment evaluates.

See the [AbsenceBench evidence and caveats](../../EVIDENCE.md#models-detect-omissions-poorly-unless-the-omission-is-marked-absencebench) and the [primary paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.11440).

**Recommended replacement:**

> In paired document-difference tasks, removed content was markedly harder for models to identify than inserted content, and marking the removal site improved performance. By analogy, a truncated result should mark the gap and explain how to retrieve the rest; the study did not directly test tool-result truncation or downstream decisions.

## 4. Principle 1 turns two bounded results into categorical claims

**Location:** v15 lines 121, 179, and 183.

The self-correction evidence supports the narrower claim that intrinsic review without external feedback can degrade reasoning performance and cannot provide the deterministic enforcement guarantee described by Principle 1. It does not establish that a model can never usefully review its own work. The cited study covers particular reasoning benchmarks and older model families, and v15 itself acknowledges domains such as tone and refusal where self-correction can work. “The verdict has to come from outside the model” should therefore be scoped to enforcement guarantees, not all review.

The runtime-policy paragraph has a separate labeling problem. Its 30% task-level intervention rate was dominated by least-privilege denials that the policy was designed to produce. That is real operational friction, but it is not the same as a measured rate of wrong predicates or refusals of valid work. Calling it “the over-enforcement cost” collapses intended capability restrictions and erroneous refusals into one category. The comparison is also from a controlled benchmark built alongside its rules; the evidence ledger records that the ordering did not hold on the paper's exploratory external benchmark.

These qualifications preserve the core argument: an unavoidable boundary check can prevent a prohibited action even when a prompt or same-model review does not. See the [self-correction evidence](../../EVIDENCE.md#intrinsic-self-correction-degraded-reasoning-an-external-verdict-improved-it), the [runtime-enforcement evidence](../../EVIDENCE.md#runtime-enforcement-outperformed-a-prompt-only-defense-for-an-mcp-agent), and the primary reports on [self-correction](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.01798) and [runtime policy enforcement](https://www.mdpi.com/2079-9292/15/13/2829).

**Recommended replacements:**

> A same-model review without independent evidence cannot supply this enforcement guarantee.

> On this controlled benchmark, full runtime enforcement reduced attack success from 40% to 5%. Its 30% task-level intervention rate represents operational friction largely caused by intended least-privilege denials, not a measured 30% policy false-positive rate.

## 5. The boundary checklist is presented as both a design tool and not a design-time filter

**Location:** v15 lines 408–416 and 458.

“How to draw the boundary” instructs designers to ask five questions in order, while Limits says that several of those questions should be treated as review questions “not as design-time filters.” The line 410 caveat—ask them again after real cases arrive—softens the conflict but does not resolve it. A reader is still told both to use the questions to draw the boundary and not to use them at design time.

The underlying idea is sound: the questions are useful before implementation, but some answers are provisional until observed cases test them. State that lifecycle directly.

**Recommended replacement:**

> Use these questions at design time as hypotheses about where the boundary belongs, then ask them again in review once real cases have arrived. Questions such as whether the model could have stated a rule in advance, or whether a predicate was the right one, cannot be considered settled by design-time reasoning alone.

## Recommended disposition

Keep v15 as the canonical base. Correct these five passages, retain the added evidence and limits, and describe the result as evidence-hardened only after the claims distinguish direct measurements from analogies and operational costs from policy errors.
