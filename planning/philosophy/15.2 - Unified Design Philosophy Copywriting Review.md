# v15.2 Copywriting Review — Unified Design Philosophy

## Verdict

This is a copy review, not a claims review. It assumes v15's arguments and evidence are settled by [v15.1](<15.1 - Unified Design Philosophy Critique.md>) and looks only at language: correctness, consistency, register, and sentence-level economy.

The prose is in good shape. The measurement discipline is the best writing in the document and should not be smoothed in the name of readability. What remains is one genuine structural break, a mixed spelling register, three unmotivated inconsistencies in how sections and terms are presented, and about a dozen line-level tightenings.

None of these change an argument. All of them are safe to apply in one pass.

Line numbers refer to the current state of [v15](<15 - Unified Design Philosophy (6 - Evidence-Hardened by Opus 5).md>).

## 1. The two-capability enumeration never delivers its second half

**Location:** lines 40, 42, 44.

Line 40 opens an enumeration: "An AI tool joins two different kinds of capability." Line 42 answers it: "The first is judgement, supplied by the AI model using the tool." Line 44 then begins "Software supplies checks and execution." — a new subject with no ordinal, so the promised second item never arrives as one.

This is the only place in the document where a stated structure is left open. It matters more than its size because it sits in the passage that defines the whole frame.

**Recommended replacement for line 44:**

> The second is checks and execution, supplied by software. It can apply a stated rule to the state it observes, refuse a prohibited change, and carry out work whose choices have already been made. Its advantage is repeatability: a check does not depend on the model remembering anything.

## 2. Spelling register is mixed between US and UK

**Locations:** lines 42, 183, 213, 251, 295, 305, 338, 457.

US spelling is the house register by a wide margin — `judgment` throughout (including the thesis blockquote at line 48), `artifact` rather than `artefact` in roughly forty places, plus `behavior` (127), `recognize` (119), `optimization` (151), `modeled` (189), `reorganizing` (393). Eight UK forms have survived against it, and two of them sit close enough to their US counterparts to read as an error rather than a choice: `modelling` (251) against `modeled` (189) in adjacent evidence blocks, and `judgement` (42) against `judgment` six lines later.

| Line | Current | Recommended |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 42 | judgement | judgment |
| 183 | defence | defense |
| 213 | colour | color |
| 251 | modelling | modeling |
| 295 | neighbouring | neighboring |
| 305 | modelling (twice) | modeling |
| 338 | defences | defenses |
| 457 | defences | defenses |

**Caveat:** apply this to the document's own prose only. Any spelling inside a direct quotation from a source keeps its original form. None of the eight above is currently inside quotation marks, but the evidence sections are where quotations get added, so the rule is worth stating before the next revision.

## 3. Section-heading verbs alternate without a reason

**Locations:** lines 141, 153, 161, 215, 229, 348, and the matching Contents entries at lines 15-17, 21-22, and 30.

Three different constructions describe the same relationship:

- "How enforcement **produces** Safer" (141)
- "How enforcement **leads to** Cleaner" (153), "...**leads to** Faster" (161)
- "How explicit structure **leads to** Safer" (215), "...**leads to** Faster" (229)
- "Why **this** produces Faster" (348)

Line 348 compounds it by naming the subject as "this" rather than the mechanism, and line 340 does the same ("How **this** works with the other three"). A reader scanning the Contents cannot tell whether "produces" and "leads to" mark a distinction the document is drawing. They do not.

**Recommendation:** pick one verb — `leads to` is already the majority — and name the mechanism in every heading, including Principle 4's. Update the four affected Contents lines in the same edit so the anchors continue to resolve.

**Related, and correct as-is:** Principle 2 has no "How explicit structure leads to Cleaner" section, because it produces Cleaner directly. That is right, but it reads as an omission next to Principle 1's full trio. One clause at the head of line 215 would close it.

## 4. Principle 4 has no "In figma-edit-mcp." paragraph

**Locations:** lines 139, 213, 289; absent from Principle 4.

Principles 1, 2, and 3 each ground themselves in the project with an identically formatted paragraph — scope and name checks (139), the bound-variable and detached-instance pairs (213), batch execution and partial-failure reporting (289). Principle 4 never does. It is the only principle argued entirely in the abstract, which is conspicuous given that it is also the principle the document says "closes the loop."

The material exists elsewhere in the repo: structured error codes, the identifiers carried in refusals, the per-item batch result rows already described at line 291. A short paragraph after line 332 would restore the pattern.

## 5. "decision-complete" is introduced without the convention used for other coinages

**Location:** line 324, against line 135 and lines 76-78.

The document bolds terms on first use — `state invariant` and `transition constraint` at line 135, `Safer` / `Cleaner` / `Faster` in their defining bullets. `decision-complete` gets no such treatment at line 324 ("We call such an exchange decision-complete"), despite being the document's only original coinage, the name of Principle 4, and a term reused nine times afterwards.

**Recommended replacement for line 324:**

> We call such an exchange **decision-complete**: it carries what the relevant decision needs, and as little else as possible. It is complete relative to a decision, not exhaustive.

## 6. Line-level tightening

**Line 5 — two ampersands and one redundant pair.** "The considerations & stakes change when the tool is capable of making changes" repeats `change`/`changes`, and these are the document's only two ampersands against `and` everywhere else. "bad writes & bad changes" names one thing twice: a bad write is a bad change.

> Much of the published guidance on designing tools for AI models assumes a tool that reads: search, retrieval, lookup. The stakes change when the tool can write. A bad read wastes a turn; a bad write damages the thing you were working on, and the damage outlives the session. This document is about avoiding bad writes.

**Line 42 — a trailing clause and a repetition.** "adapts when new information changes what the task it was assigned means" unwinds backwards; "Instructions can improve that judgment, but they cannot guarantee that the AI model will follow the instructions correctly every time" uses `instructions` twice in one sentence.

> The first is judgment, supplied by the AI model using the tool. The AI model interprets intent, resolves ambiguity, chooses among valid alternatives, and adapts when new information changes what the task means. Instructions can improve that judgment, but they cannot guarantee the model follows them every time.

**Line 139 — a comma-spliced question list, a person shift, and a register drop.** Five checks run as comma-separated questions with no question marks or semicolons: "is the target inside the area you are working in, is it really the layer the model named, does a new layer have somewhere to go, is the layer locked, does this variable still have things using it." The first item switches to second person in a sentence otherwise about the model, and the last drops to "things using it" from a paragraph that elsewhere says "consumers."

> Each check enforces one rule on every action: is the target inside the working area; is it really the layer the model named; does a new layer have somewhere to go; is the layer locked; does this variable still have consumers.

**Line 183 — the densest paragraph in the document.** A 45-word sentence ("Its 30.0% task-level intervention rate is operational friction [...] whatever its cause") is followed immediately by a 38-word one ("The bound matters as much as the result [...] rather than to a better check"). Both carry load placed there by v15.1 finding 4, so neither can be cut — but they can be split. The paragraph also repeats its verb: "brought that to 35.0%" then "brought it to 5.0%." Change the second to "cut it to 5.0%."

**Line 189 — "IBM's original inspection study."** `original` has no antecedent; no other IBM study is mentioned. Drop it.

**Line 297 — "Read the timing with its filter."** `filter` is doing the work of `caveat` and does not carry it. The sentence that follows is about a restriction on the sample, so: "Read the timing with that restriction in mind."

**Line 303 — "observed in both directions by one mechanism."** `by` misassigns the agent; the mechanism did not do the observing.

> That is the crossover this principle predicts, observed in both directions within a single mechanism.

## 7. Register and person

**Person shifts three ways.** The document uses impersonal third person as its base, drops into second person for instruction ("Write the instruction from the predicate" at 147, "your tool" at 433, "Read a transcript" at 439), and uses first-person plural exactly twice — the section title at 38 and "We call such an exchange" at 324.

The second-person passages are deliberate and effective: they cluster in the two how-to sections and read as direct address to a designer. The two `we` instances are the odd ones, because nothing else in the document establishes a first-person voice. Either commit to it or convert both.

**"the file" against "the artifact."** `artifact` is the chosen general noun, correctly, because the argument is meant to reach past Figma. Two general passages nevertheless slip to `file`: line 46 ("standing between a prohibited action and the file") and line 157 ("Think of the errors in a file as a level"). Both are in principle-level prose, not in the Figma examples where `file` is right.

**Bold lead-ins vary in grammatical form within a section.** Principle 2's evidence lead-ins are four bare noun phrases and scan cleanly (251-257). Principle 1's are five noun phrases plus one full sentence at 179. Principle 4's mix noun phrases (356, 358, 362) with a sentence (360) and an imperative (364). Choose one form per section.

**The Limits list changes shape two thirds of the way down.** Lines 449-456 are eight terse single-clause bullets; 457 and 458 are multi-sentence bullets with bold lead-ins. The last two are longer because they carry more, which is fine, but the bold lead-ins appear nowhere else in the list and make them look like a different category of limit.

## 8. Notes that are not defects

- **Keep `5.7x` at line 301.** This file's non-ASCII budget is `—` and `→`; do not "improve" it to a multiplication sign.
- **The `27/40` to `15/40` fraction at line 358** is correct as reported and should not be converted to percentages.
- **Mixed decimal precision** across the evidence sections (`40.0%` beside `76%`) reflects the sources and should stay.
- **"projected 24% share by 2024" at line 187** was a forward-looking figure when written and now reads stale in a document dated 2026. This is an evidence question rather than a copy one, but the tense is a copy problem either way: `on the way to a projected 24% share by 2024` should either become the actual figure or be marked as a projection made at the time.
- **The trailing em-dash qualifier is this document's signature sentence shape** — a claim, then a dash, then the restriction on the claim (183, 299, 301, 303, 305, 363). It is the right shape for evidence-hardened prose and should not be eliminated. It does stack: lines 299 and 305 each use it twice. Converting one instance per paragraph to a following sentence would relieve it without changing the register.

## What to leave alone

The following is the strongest copy in the document and should survive any edit pass:

- The separation of wall-clock time, task time, model-inference latency, and elapsed time, and the refusal at line 456 to treat them as interchangeable.
- "percentage points" used correctly at lines 301 and 356.
- "Trim successes; do not trim refusals." (328)
- "Checks do not lower the level; they slow what raises it." (157)
- "A tool that fails cleanly and a tool that fails halfway through score the same and are not the same tool." (437)
- "Success rates measure the cases you thought of; the checks exist for the ones you did not." (443)

## Recommended disposition

Apply sections 1 through 6 in a single pass; they are mechanical and carry no argumentative risk. Section 3 requires touching the Contents block in the same edit. Section 4 is the only item that needs new prose rather than a correction. Treat section 7 as a style decision to be made once and applied throughout, and section 8 as a do-not-touch list for whoever runs the next edit.
